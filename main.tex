\begin{abstract}
Long-lived language agents accumulate facts that change, and answering a multi-hop question
requires not only chaining hops but also choosing the current version of each fact along the chain.
Existing memories either rely on LLM-issued updates whose evidence can be missing or wrong, or treat
version selection as a post-retrieval decision that exposes the reader to order-sensitive pairwise
comparisons. We observe that spiking neural circuits face a structurally similar
problem---selecting among competing, partially reliable inputs---and solve it with a small set of
motifs. \sm{} composes three of them into an agent memory: a coincidence threshold gates each hop on
subject and relation jointly, conduction delays count hops, and winner-take-all competition with
finite, directed supersede inhibition arbitrates between versions \emph{before} activity propagates to
the next hop. Because inhibition is finite rather than masking, a fact wrongly marked as expired is
weakened but not removed. On a MuSiQue-derived benchmark with superseded bridge entities and ten
settings of update-evidence noise, \sm{} is the most accurate of nine memories in every setting,
leading retrieval-augmented baselines by $0.35$ or more while calling the reader about a ninth as
often. Ablations show that version arbitration is indispensable and that finite inhibition is what
supplies robustness to false expiration signals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Long-lived language agents accumulate facts that change. A user moves, a company changes its CEO,
a document is revised. Answering a multi-hop question such as ``Which country is the current
employer of X's spouse based in?'' then requires two things at every hop: finding the fact that links
the current entity to the next, and choosing the \emph{current} version when several versions of that
fact are stored. Agent memory systems address the second requirement with add/update/delete
operations \citep{chhikara2025mem0} or temporally scoped graph edges \citep{rasmussen2025zep}, but in
deployment the evidence that one fact supersedes another is itself inferred, and can be missing (a
stale fact still looks current) or spurious (a current fact looks expired).

Neural circuits face a structurally similar problem---selecting one interpretation among competing,
partially reliable inputs---and solve it with a small set of motifs: coincidence detection,
conduction delay, lateral inhibition, and winner-take-all competition
\citep{shastri1993shruti,maass2000wta}. \sm{} uses these motifs as the design language for an agent
memory. Each stored fact is a leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) unit. A fact fires only when its subject
and relation are activated together, so each hop is a conjunction; conduction delays place the $k$-th
hop at a known time, so hop depth needs no explicit counter; and facts that fill the same
(subject, relation) slot compete, with newer versions inhibiting older ones through edges written at
insertion time. A key design choice is that this inhibition is \emph{finite} rather than masking:
a fact wrongly marked as expired is weakened, not removed, and can still win when it is the only
candidate that completes the chain. As we show in \S\ref{sec:ablation}, switching to masking
inhibition reproduces exactly the accuracy of hard invalidation under false-positive noise, so
finiteness is what buys robustness.

Because the circuit settles on a single winning chain before read-out, it also sidesteps a problem
we did not initially set out to solve. A memory that returns two candidate chains forces the reader
into a pairwise decision that, for current LLM readers, is strongly order-sensitive
\citep{wang2024fair,zheng2023judge}: $30.5\%$ of the two-chain decisions in our evaluation change
answer when the presentation order is reversed. A memory that arbitrates internally asks the reader to
make fewer such decisions. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} quantifies this downstream effect.

We evaluate \sm{} end to end on MuSiQue-derived multi-hop questions with superseded bridge entities,
ten settings of update-evidence noise, and a sweep over update prevalence, against
retrieval-augmented, iterative, temporal knowledge-graph, and graph-propagation memories
(\S\ref{sec:setup}). All methods share one frozen reader and an order-balanced scoring protocol that
accounts for the reader's position bias. \sm{} leads all baselines in every setting
(\S\ref{sec:results}), and ablations attribute the margin to version arbitration---indispensable---and
to finite inhibition---the source of noise robustness (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Agent memory under updates.}
Mem0 \citep{chhikara2025mem0} maintains memory through LLM-issued add, update, and delete operations;
Zep \citep{rasmussen2025zep} stores temporally scoped knowledge-graph edges; LongMemEval
\citep{wu2025longmemeval} and MemoryAgentBench \citep{hu2026memoryagentbench} evaluate knowledge
updates, and \citet{yadav2026temporal} show that retaining superseded history without supersession is
severely damaging. Temporal knowledge-graph QA answers multi-hop questions over time-stamped facts
\citep{saxena2021cronkgqa}. \sm{} differs in performing arbitration inside the retrieval dynamics, hop
by hop, and in tolerating noisy supersession evidence through finite inhibition.

\paragraph{Multi-hop retrieval.}
Iterative retrieve-and-reason interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought
\citep{trivedi2023interleaving,press2023measuring}; we compare against a simpler, LLM-free iterative
retriever that follows one fact per hop. HippoRAG applies personalized PageRank over an entity graph
\citep{gutierrez2024hipporag}, which we include as a baseline.

\paragraph{Spiking associative memory.}
Temporal synchrony for variable binding originates with \citet{shastri1993shruti}; winner-take-all
computation and spike-timing plasticity are classical \citep{maass2000wta,caporale2008stdp}. We claim
no novelty for these components; the contribution is their composition into an agent memory for
multi-hop reasoning over updated facts. Spiking graph algorithms have provable advantages in
neuromorphic hardware cost models \citep{aimone2020neuromorphic}; we do not evaluate on such hardware.

\paragraph{Reader-side position bias.}
LLM judges and readers prefer candidates by position, and fair comparison requires evaluating both
orders \citep{wang2024fair,zheng2023judge,shi2024judges}. More retrieved context can also hurt
\citep{hsia2024ragged,cuconasu2024noise}. We adopt order-balanced scoring throughout, and
\S\ref{sec:analysis} shows why it matters for comparing memories.

\section{\sm}
\label{sec:method}

\sm{} stores each fact as a spiking neuron in a graph. When a multi-hop question arrives, the entry
retriever activates a small set of candidate first-hop facts. Each activated unit integrates input
from the query and, if its subject and relation are both present, fires and sends excitation along
its out-edge to the next hop's candidates. At every hop, facts that fill the same slot compete
through supersede inhibition, so only the version that wins the competition propagates further.
After a fixed number of hops the circuit settles, and the terminal value of the surviving chain is
read out---usually without calling the reader at all. Three circuit motifs make this work.

\paragraph{Memory representation.}
Memory is a graph of localist fact units $m_i=(s_i,p_i,v_i,d_i,c_i,z_i)$: subject, predicate, value,
write order, confidence, and validity state. When a write $s\xrightarrow{p}v_{\text{new}}$ fills a
slot $\sigma=(s,p)$ already holding $v_{\text{old}}$, a directed supersede edge from the new unit to
the old one is created at write time. Direction is taken from the write stream because read-time
co-activation is symmetric and cannot identify which version is newer.

\paragraph{Dynamics.}
Each fact unit is a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron,
\begin{align}
V_i(t) = {}& \beta V_i(t-1) + \alpha\,g(q,m_i) \nonumber\\
 & + \textstyle\sum_j w_{ij}\,s_j(t-\delta_{ij}) \nonumber\\
 & - \textstyle\sum_j \mu_{ij}\,s_j(t-\delta_{\text{sup}}),
\end{align}
firing when $V_i(t)\ge\theta_i$, where $g(q,m_i)$ is query similarity, $w_{ij}$ are excitatory edges
from upstream facts, and $\mu_{ij}$ are supersede inhibitions.

\paragraph{Conjunctive hop gating.}
The first motif enforces a conjunction at every hop.
With single-input postsynaptic peak $A$ and $A<\theta_i<2A$, no single input fires a unit: subject and
relation activation must coincide within $\tau_c=\ln(\theta_i/A-1)/\ln\beta$, which is positive
because numerator and denominator are both negative. Each hop is thus an AND over subject and
relation, which rejects facts that match only one of them. This is the motif that separates \sm{}
from flat retrieval: a one-shot retriever must find every hop's fact from the question text alone,
whereas the AND gate lets \sm{} follow bridge entities that do not appear in the question.

\paragraph{Hop counting via conduction delay.}
The second motif replaces an explicit hop counter with timing.
A per-hop conduction delay $\delta_h$ places hop-$k$ facts near $k\delta_h$, so a read-out window
selects chains of the decomposed length and rejects under-length shortcuts.

\paragraph{Version arbitration with finite inhibition.}
The third motif---and the one that ablations will show is indispensable---handles version competition.
Facts in the same slot compete through a winner-take-all slot node, and supersede edges inhibit older
versions. The timing constraint $\delta_\sigma+\delta_{\text{sup}}<\delta_{\text{out}}$ ensures that a
superseded fact is suppressed \emph{before} its outgoing edge excites the next hop, so arbitration
happens before propagation rather than after.

Why finite inhibition rather than masking? A masking rule deletes any fact that
receives supersede inhibition, which works perfectly when the supersede evidence is correct. But
under false-positive noise---a valid fact erroneously marked as expired---masking permanently removes
a fact that should have won. Finite inhibition weakens it instead, so that if no competing version
completes the chain, the weakened fact can still propagate. As we quantify in
\S\ref{sec:ablation}, this single design choice accounts for the entire gap between \sm{} and hard
invalidation under false-positive noise.

\paragraph{Arbitrated read-out.}
When arbitration leaves exactly one complete chain within the hop window, its terminal value is read
out directly, without calling the reader. When it cannot decide---for instance, when noise has
removed a supersede edge so that two versions both remain valid---the two earliest complete chains
are passed to the reader. Under our noise settings this happens for $0$ to $28\%$ of questions.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
We simulate the network with an event-driven scheduler. The same computations can be executed as an
event-driven graph search with an explicit AND filter, hop counter, and sparse arbitration; on all
$24{,}170$ query--setting pairs of our evaluation this produces identical outputs. We therefore
attribute \sm{}'s behaviour to the computations its circuit performs rather than to spike timing as
such, and regard the spiking form as the design language and an implementation suited to
event-driven hardware, which we do not evaluate here.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
We adapt the $2{,}417$ answerable questions of the MuSiQue development split
\citep{trivedi2022musique}, whose per-hop decompositions give full gold chains of length
$K\in\{2,3,4\}$ without entity-recognition heuristics. With probability $\rho$ ($0.5$ unless stated)
a bridge slot is updated: an old value, drawn from a genuine MuSiQue distractor title rather than
synthesised, is written before the new one, and the gold chain continues from the new value.

\paragraph{Update-evidence noise.}
Supersede evidence is corrupted in two ways at rates $0.1$, $0.2$, and $0.3$: \emph{false positive}
(\textsc{fp}), a valid fact is marked expired; \emph{false negative} (\textsc{fn}), a real supersede
edge is dropped so that stale and current versions both appear valid. Three diagonal settings
($\textsc{fp}=\textsc{fn}$) and a clean setting give ten configurations. Injection and noise are
query-scoped, and we verified that neither leaks across questions nor destroys chains by construction
(Appendix~\ref{sec:selfchecks}).

\paragraph{Entry.}
All methods enter the graph with the same sparse TF--IDF retriever over the question and entity
titles, using no gold seed, answer, path, or support annotation (full-dev seed@1 $=0.6856$).

\paragraph{Reader and scoring.}
A frozen Qwen3-8B reader (greedy decoding, non-thinking mode, at most 16 new tokens) sees the question
and the rendered candidate chains with write orders, and may answer \texttt{INSUFFICIENT}; it never
sees gold labels or noise types. A single candidate chain is read out directly. Every two-candidate
decision is run in both presentation orders and scored as the mean of the two correctness indicators
\citep{wang2024fair,zheng2023judge}. Retrieval-augmented baselines give the reader a list of $k$ facts,
each rendered as \texttt{subject | predicate | value (write order d)}; the list is presented in
descending and in ascending retrieval-score order and the two scores are averaged. All methods are
scored with the same function, normalized exact match: an answer is correct if, after lower-casing and
removing punctuation, articles, and extra whitespace, it equals the gold answer or one of its MuSiQue
aliases; direct read-outs and reader outputs are scored identically. Every number in this paper comes
from a single from-scratch run of graph construction, retrieval, and reader generation; under strict
string matching it agrees with an earlier independent run within $0.002$ in $33$ of $35$ compared cells;
the two larger differences ($0.003$ and $0.006$) come from a deterministic tie-breaking rule adopted for
the rerun and from a handful of reader outputs that changed for byte-identical prompts.
Differences use a paired bootstrap over questions with $2{,}000$ resamples.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
All methods share the $11{,}322$-fact graph and the reader; graph methods also share the entry
retriever. None was tuned on the evaluation questions.
\emph{Dense RAG}: the top-$k$ facts by cosine similarity between \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}
embeddings of the question and the facts.
\emph{BM25 RAG}: the top-$k$ facts by BM25 ($k_1=1.5$, $b=0.75$).
\emph{Recency-deduplicated RAG}: the dense top-$2k$, keeping only the currently valid fact with the
latest write order in each (subject, predicate) slot, then the top $k$; it uses the same
(noisy) validity and write-order information as \sm{}.
We report $k=8$ in the main text and $k=4$ in Appendix~\ref{sec:k4}.
\emph{Iterative}: an LLM-free hop-by-hop retriever that follows the best-matching fact from the
current entity at each hop and uses no validity or supersede information.
\emph{Temporal KG (hard / soft)}: one-shot search over a temporally scoped graph in the style of
\citet{rasmussen2025zep}, with hard invalidation (expired facts removed) or soft invalidation
(expired facts down-weighted), enumerating up to two complete chains from a top-16 pool.
\emph{Personalized PageRank}: sparse PPR over the fact graph in the style of
\citet{gutierrez2024hipporag}.

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports end-to-end accuracy across all ten noise settings. Two patterns stand
out: the gap between memories that follow bridges and those that do not, and the gap between
different strategies for handling version competition under noise.

\paragraph{Following bridges is necessary but not sufficient.}
Retrieval-augmented baselines and personalized PageRank never exceed $0.31$, regardless of noise
setting---their scores are flat across all rows of Table~\ref{tab:main} because they ignore
validity information entirely. The bottleneck is structural: a one-shot retriever must find every
hop's fact from the question text alone, and bridge entities introduced by updates do not appear in
the question. Even recency-deduplicated RAG, which uses the same validity and write-order
information available to \sm{}, gains only about $0.04$ over plain dense retrieval, because
deduplication cannot help a retriever that never reaches the bridge in the first place. Sparse PPR
scores $0.019$: single-pass diffusion followed by ranking does not enforce the per-hop conjunctions
these questions require. The iterative retriever does follow bridges hop by hop, but without
version arbitration it propagates from whichever version it encounters first, whether current or
stale.

\paragraph{Version arbitration under noise separates \sm{} from temporal KG memories.}
The temporal KG baselines do follow bridges and do arbitrate versions, and on clean data hard
invalidation ties \sm{} at $0.6856$---both answer every question their entry retriever reaches. The
difference emerges under noise, and it tracks the kind of noise in exactly the way the design
predicts.

Under false-positive noise, where valid facts are erroneously marked expired, hard invalidation
permanently removes facts that should have won; its accuracy falls from $0.6856$ to $0.5445$ at
\textsc{fp}$=0.3$, a loss of $0.141$. \sm{}'s finite inhibition weakens those facts instead of
deleting them, so it loses only $0.030$ over the same range. Soft invalidation keeps falsely
expired facts (as \sm{} does), so it degrades less than hard---but it trails \sm{} for a different
reason that \S\ref{sec:analysis} isolates: it passes more candidate chains to the reader, whose
order-sensitive decisions cost accuracy.

Under false-negative noise, where real supersede edges are dropped, both hard invalidation and \sm{}
lose the information they would need to distinguish versions; the margins are small ($+0.001$ to
$+0.008$). In diagonal settings, where both kinds of noise are present, the false-positive effect
dominates and \sm{} leads by up to $+0.136$.

Across all settings, \sm{} degrades most gracefully: from clean to diagonal $0.3$ it loses $0.050$,
against $0.186$ for hard and $0.067$ for soft invalidation.

\paragraph{Where the remaining error is.}
On clean data \sm{} answers every question it enters correctly: its $0.6856$ equals the entry
retriever's seed@1, and all $760$ misses are questions whose entry fact was not retrieved. Under noise
its gold coverage stays between $0.672$ and $0.686$ while that of hard invalidation falls to $0.517$.
The entry retriever, not the memory, is \sm{}'s main limitation on this benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} strips components one at a time, and the pattern that emerges is a clear
hierarchy of necessity.

\emph{Removing arbitration} collapses accuracy to $0.2503$ in all three settings---the same score as
the iterative baseline, including on clean data where half the bridges have been updated. Without
version competition, stale and current branches both propagate, and the chain that completes first
need not be the current one. Arbitration is indispensable.

\emph{Switching to masking inhibition} reveals where robustness comes from. On clean data and under
false-negative noise, masking is equivalent to \sm{}---both settings provide correct or absent
supersede signals, so deleting an inhibited fact costs nothing. But under \textsc{fp}$=0.3$, masking
falls to $0.5445$, exactly the accuracy of hard invalidation in Table~\ref{tab:main}: falsely
expired facts are permanently removed, and the chain they would have completed is lost. The entire
gap between \sm{} and hard invalidation under false-positive noise ($+0.111$) is accounted for by
this single design choice---finite versus masking inhibition.

\emph{Forcing top-2 read-out}---always passing two chains to the reader when both exist---leaves
accuracy unchanged, because after arbitration a second complete chain exists only where \sm{} already
defers to the reader. What matters for read-out is not a policy of sending two chains, but whether
arbitration has already settled the competition; \S\ref{sec:analysis} makes this point from the
opposite direction.

Figure~\ref{fig:rho} varies the fraction of updated bridge slots under combined noise. All methods

\section{Where does the margin over soft invalidation come from?}
\label{sec:analysis}

Soft invalidation keeps falsely expired facts, as \sm{} does, yet it trails \sm{} in every setting.
The reason is not what each memory knows but what each memory hands the reader.

Soft invalidation enumerates two complete chains for
$40$--$44\%$ of questions in every setting, forcing the reader to choose between them. \sm{}
arbitrates inside the memory and passes two chains for at most $28\%$ of questions, and for none on
clean data. To isolate this effect, we take the \textsc{fp}$=0.3$ setting on an $N=800$ slice with
the historical annotated entry and give the soft baseline the full graph as its pool, which brings
its gold coverage (\goldtwo{}) to \sm{}'s $0.975$. \sm{} still scores $0.9519$ against $0.8988$
(paired 95\% CI of the difference $[0.0425, 0.0644]$). The difference is concentrated in $343$
questions where both memories contain the identical gold fact path: \sm{} returns that chain alone,
soft invalidation returns it together with one distractor chain.

Table~\ref{tab:readout} makes this precise by artificially adding a distractor chain to \sm{}'s
output on exactly these $343$ questions. Adding a second chain that agrees on the answer costs
nothing; adding the soft baseline's actual distractor reproduces the baseline's accuracy: in $85$
of the $343$ the reader's answer flips with presentation order, costing exactly half a point each,
$85\times0.5/800=0.0531$. The reader never consistently prefers the wrong chain---it simply picks
whichever chain is presented first.

For memory design, the implication is that settling competition inside the memory is itself a source
of end-to-end accuracy with current readers. It also follows that retrieval coverage alone, such as
\goldtwo{}, understates differences between memories, and we recommend reporting candidate
cardinality and reader-invocation rate alongside it.

\section{Conclusion}

\sm{} builds an agent memory from three spiking circuit motifs---coincidence gating, conduction
delay, and winner-take-all arbitration with finite, write-time supersede inhibition---and uses them
to perform version arbitration inside the search process, hop by hop. The experimental record
attributes its margin to two sources: version arbitration itself, without which accuracy collapses
to the iterative baseline, and the finiteness of inhibition, which preserves facts that were
wrongly marked as expired.

A broader design lesson emerges from the analysis of read-out. Current LLM readers are strongly
order-sensitive in pairwise decisions, and a memory that settles competition before read-out spares
the reader those decisions entirely. This suggests that for any agent memory, not just spiking ones,
moving arbitration earlier in the pipeline---from reader-side to retrieval-side---is worth exploring
as a general strategy, especially as memories grow and the number of competing versions increases.

On this benchmark, \sm{}'s remaining errors are almost entirely due to the entry retriever, not the
memory. Improving entry---whether through denser retrieval, query expansion, or entity
linking---is the most direct path to higher end-to-end accuracy.

\section*{Limitations}

Updates and supersession noise are injected into MuSiQue rather than naturally occurring, and the fact
graph is built from its annotated decompositions; results on naturally updated memories may differ.
Retrieval-augmented baselines must extract the answer from a list of facts in free text, whereas graph
memories often return an entity directly; normalized matching with aliases reduces but may not
remove this asymmetry. Our iterative baseline is LLM-free, and LLM-driven iterative retrieval
\citep{trivedi2023interleaving} and LLM-maintained memories \citep{chhikara2025mem0} are not included.
Results use one reader, Qwen3-8B; the read-out component of the margin depends
on that reader's order sensitivity and may shrink for readers that adjudicate pairs reliably. The
reader never used its \texttt{INSUFFICIENT} option even when neither candidate was correct
(Appendix~\ref{sec:abstain}), so abstention is not an effective safeguard in this pipeline. Because
\sm{} is equivalent to an event-driven graph search with the same components, we make no claim that
spike timing itself improves accuracy, and we did not measure energy or latency on neuromorphic
hardware. In separate exploratory work on FactConsolidation-MH \citep{hu2026memoryagentbench}, a
second spiking design that bridged hops without an LLM did not reach the coverage of an LLM-bridged
pipeline; \sm{}'s design should not be assumed to transfer to benchmarks where bridging between hops,
rather than version arbitration, is the bottleneck.

\section{Benchmark self-checks}
\label{sec:selfchecks}

\paragraph{Query-scoped injection.}
Because MuSiQue entities are shared across questions, an update injected for one question could alter
retrieval for others. We scope both staleness information and predicates to their question and check
the $878$ questions that injection did not touch: their fraction, $0.3633$, matches its analytic value
$0.3586$, and on them every graph memory scores exactly the entry retriever's seed@1 on that subset,
$0.6891$, in all ten settings.
Neither injection nor noise leaks across questions.

\paragraph{Finite inhibition.}
If noise deterministically destroyed any chain it touched, every method would score the probability
that no bridge was touched and all methods would tie; with chain-length mixture weights and per-bridge
noise rate $p$, that probability is $\mathbb{E}_K[(1-\rho p)^{K-1}]$. \sm{} and our benchmark therefore
use finite inhibition, under which noise is recoverable, and the masking ablation in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows the cost of the alternative.

Figure~\ref{fig:flip} shows the order-flip rate across settings. Over the $14{,}293$ two-chain

a comparison between memories in either direction, which is why all numbers in this paper are
order-balanced.

\section{Abstention}
\label{sec:abstain}

On the full-dev \textsc{fp}$=0.3$ soft-invalidation run, $72$ two-chain cases contain no correct
answer in either candidate; the reader abstains in none of them, in either order. The same holds for
$22$ cases under \textsc{fn}$=0.3$ and $113$ under diagonal $0.3$. This agrees with prior findings
that offering an abstention option does not by itself produce abstention.
